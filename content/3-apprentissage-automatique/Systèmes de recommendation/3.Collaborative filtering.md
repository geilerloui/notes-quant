\section{Collaborative Filtering}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\subsection{Introduction }
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Definition (Collaborative Filtering).} We will consider a user $x$ and a group of $N$ other users whose ratings are "similar" to $x$'s ratings. Typically if you do movie recommendation they will like and dislikes the same types of movies. We call this set of users the neighborhood of $x$. Next we find other movies that are liked by a lot of users and we recommend them to user $x$. The key trick is to find the neighborhood of user $x$.

![[intro-1 1.png|211]]



\textbf{Complexity.} The most expensive step is finding the $\mathbf{k}$ most similar users (or items). Typically of size
$$
\sim O(|U|)
$$
Where $|U|$ is the size of the utility matrix. Alternatives could be considered as the Nearest-Neighbor search in high dimension (LSH), Clustering or Dimensionality Reduction.\\


\textbf{Pros and cons.} The pros are summarized in one sentence - it works for any kind of items, no feature selection is needed.\\

In contrast the cons are multiple, 

\begin{itemize}
    \item (i) Cold start - we need enough users in the system to find a match 
    \item (ii) Sparsity - The user/ratings matrix is sparse, as such, it is hard to find users that have rated the same items.
    \item (iii) First rater - We cannot recommend an unrated item. 
    \item (iv) Popularity bias (aka the Harry "Potter Effect" - It tends to recommend popular items, mostly because if a movie or book is well known most users will rate it well. Which implies that the CF will recommend it to anyone.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{User Similarity - User-User CF}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Motivation.} Consider users $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ with rating vectors $\mathbf{r}_X$ and $\mathbf{r}_Y$. We need to define a similarity metric $sim(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})$ that captures the intuition that $sim(A,B) > sim(A,C)$ as in the matrix below.

![[memory-1.png]]


\textbf{\underline{Option 1: Jaccard Similarity}.}\\

\textbf{Definition.} The jaccard similarity is defined as:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sim}(\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B})=\left|r_{\mathrm{A}} \cap r_{\mathrm{B}}\right| /\left|r_{\mathrm{A}} \cup r_{\mathrm{B}}\right|}
$$
\textbf{Example.} In the example matrix we note that between the users $A$ and $B$ there are only one common movie and five in total. Thus we get $sim(A,B)= 1/5$. By the same reasoning we can say that $sim(A,C) = 2/4$ which implies that $sim(A,B) < sim(A,C)$ which is the opposite to what we stated in the motivation paragraph.\\

\textbf{Drawbacks.} It does not take into account how much users liked the movies. And it doesn't follow our initial intuition for the similarities.\\



\textbf{\underline{Option 2: Cosine Similarity}.}\\

\textbf{Definition.} The cosine similarity is defined as:
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sim}(A, B)=\cos \left(r_{A}, r_{B}\right)}
$$
\textbf{Example.} To compute the cosine similarity we have first to replace all unknown values by the zero value. Similarly to option 1 we get  $\operatorname{sim}(\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B})=0.38, \operatorname{sim}(\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{C})=0.32$. It captures the fact that $sim(A,B) > sim(A,C)$ but it not by much.\\

\textbf{Drawbacks.} Is that it treats missing ratings as negative ratings. We have used zero to fill in the blanket but in our ratings from 0 to 5, 0 is the worst possible rating. We have sort of assumed that if a user has not rating a movie it gave to it a rating of zero which is a bad assumption especially with user $A$ who likes Harry Potter 1.\\

\newpage
\textbf{\underline{Option 3: Centered cosine aka Pearson Correlation}.}\\

\textbf{Definition.} To solve the core issue of Cosine similarity we will normalize the ratings by subtracting the row mean. Then compute the similarity by the cosine.
$$
\boxed{\operatorname{sim}(A, B)=\cos \left(r_{A}, r_{B}\right)}
$$

\textbf{Example.} For the first row we get $10/3$ then the second row $14/3$ thus we get an updated table.

![[memory-2.png]]



We can notice that the rating for star wars has become a negative rating. And of course we treat the blank values as zeroes. On top of that note that each rows now sum to zero, zero is now the average rating. So that now a positive rating means that a user liked a movie than average and a negative rating that he liked it less than average.\\

Finally we compute this transformed matrix with the cosine similarity $\operatorname{sim}(\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{B})=\cos \left(r_{\mathrm{A}}, \mathrm{r}_{\mathrm{B}}\right)=0.09 ; \operatorname{sim}(\mathrm{A}, \mathrm{C})=-0.56$,, now we have a big gap between the two similarity. We have now captured the property that $sim(A,B) > sim(A,C)$\\

\textbf{Advantages.} It treats the missing rating as "average". Also it is a nice way to handle "tough raters" and "easy raters". That is users that always give bad scores and those that do the opposite. \\


%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
\textbf{\underline{Rating Predictions}.}\\
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%


Let $\mathbf{r}_X$ be the vector of user $\mathbf{x}$'s ratings. Let $\mathbf{N}$ be the set of $\mathbf{k}$ users most similar to $\mathbf{x}$ who have also rated item $\mathbf{i}$. The prediction for user $\mathbf{x}$ and item $\mathbf{i}$ can be two options:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\text{Option 1.} \quad r_{\mathbf{xi}} = \frac{1}{k} \sum_{y \in N} r_{yi} \\
&\text{Option 2.} \quad r_{\mathbf{xi}} = \frac{\sum_{y \in N} s_{xy} r_{yi}}{\sum_{y \in N} s_{xy}}
\end{aligned}
$$
Where $s_{xy} = sim(x,y)$

\subsubsection{Item-Item CF}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Motivation.} So far we have focused on the User-User collaborative filtering. Another view is the Item-Item CF, that is, for an item $\mathbf{i}$ we want to find other similar items. And estimate the rating for the item $\mathbf{i}$ based on the ratings of similar items. To do that, we can use the same similarity metrics and prediction functions as in the user-user model
$$
\boxed{r_{xi} = \frac{\sum_{j \in N(i;x)} s_{ij} \cdot r_{xj}}{\sum_{j \in N(i;x)} s_{ij}}}
$$
Where $s_{ij}$ is the similarity of items $i$ and $j$. $r_{xj}$ is the rating of user $x$ on item $j$. And $N(i;x)$ is the set of items rated by $x$ similar to $i$.\\

\textbf{Example.} We set the neighborhood size as $|N|=2$, we look at the two nearest neighbor of item $i$. We have plotted the utility matrix of movies/users relationship, in blank we have the unknown ratings and in yellow the rating between 1 to 5. Our goal is to estimate the rating of movie 1 of user 5 in red.\\

The first step is to take movie one and find other movie that are similar to it, we do by that the pearson correlation. Recall the two steps of the correlation (i) We substract the mean rating $m_i$ from each movie $i$ that is $m_1=(1+3+5+5+4)/5$ thus the row $1$ becomes $[-2.6,0,-0.6,0,0,1.4,0,0,1.4,0,0.4,0]$ (ii) We compute the cosine similarities between rows. Finally we note that the two most similar movies are the movies three and six.


![[memory-3.png]]


Secondly, we apply the weighted average formula
$$
r_{15} = \frac{(0.41 \times 2 + 0.59 \times 3)}{(0.41 + 0.59)} = 2.6
$$
Thus, the missing rating is $2.6$.\\

\textbf{Item-Item vs User-User.} In theory, those two approaches are dual of each other. Although in practice, item-item generally outperforms the user-user in many use cases. Typically the key property that items have is that they are simpler than users. They do belong to a small set of "genres", users have varied tastes. Item similarity is more meaningful than User similarity.

\subsubsection{Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Definition (SVD for RS).} Recall the formula of SVD
$$
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U} \Sigma \mathbf{V}^{\mathbf{T}}
$$
We can think of the elements of this matrix as the "Concepts" aka latent dimensions aka latent factors. \\

\textbf{Input.} In row we have a set of users which are divided into those that enjoys SciFi movies and those that prefers Romance movies.

![[svd-1.png]]


Next by plug in the matrix into a python code we get 


![[svd-2.png]]

There are three elements to consider:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$U$ user-to-concept matrix.} On the row we have the users and on the column the "concepts". That is a summary of the categories of the movies. We say that the last column is noise
    \item \textbf{$\Sigma$ Strength of every concept.} It tells us that the concept sciFi is the most important
    \item \textbf{$V$ movie-to-concept matrix.} On the columln we have the movies and on the rows the concepts. We argue that the last row is noise
\end{itemize}






\newpage
\textbf{How to reduce the dimensionality ?} It suffices to remove the row/column which has the lowest singular value.


![[svd-5.png]]




\textbf{How to query ?} We can project a query $q$ (a new user) into the concept space by projection. Compactly we have
$$
q_{concept} = q \cdot V
$$
The output yields the strength of each concept for a particular query vector. Here the Sci-fi concept stands out of the matrix factorization.

![[svd-3.png]]


We can note below an interesting fact: Even though user $d$ and $q$ have no ratings in common they do are similar in terms of concepts.


![[svd-4.png]]

\subsubsection{$QP$ Factorization}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%


\textbf{Definition (QP factorization).} For now let's assume we can approximate the rating matrix $R$ as a product of 
$$
R \approx Q \cdot P^T
$$


![[QP-1.png]]


\textbf{Prediction.} To estimate one missing value we define
$$
\hat{r}_{xi} = q_i \cdot p_x^T = \sum_{k} q_{ik} \cdot p_{xk}
$$
Where $q_i$ is the row $i$ of $Q$ and $p_x$ is the column $x$ of $P^T$


![[QP-2.png]]

\textbf{Algorithm.} We know that SVD is not defined when entries are missing. So we will compute the reconstruction error on the training set
$$
\boxed{\min _{P, Q} f(P,Q)= \sum_{(i, x) \in R}\left(r_{x i}-q_{i} \cdot p_{x}^{T}\right)^{2} + \lambda\left[\sum_{x}\left\|p_{x}\right\|^{2}+\sum_{i}\left\|q_{i}\right\|^{2}\right]}
$$
To update the equation we will need gradient descent, we compute the derivatives
$$
\nabla q_{ik} = \sum_{xi} -2 (r_{xi} - q_i p_x^T) p_{xk} + 2 \lambda q_{ik}
$$
And similarly for $\nabla p_{ik}$. Next we update by gradient descent
\begin{itemize}
\item Initialize $P$ and $Q$ Then do gradient descent
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P \leftarrow P - \eta \cdot \nabla P$
    \item $Q \leftarrow Q - \eta \cdot \nabla Q$
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Expectation Maximization}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%


\textbf{(i) Initialization.} We build on top of a rating matrix of $1200$ observations and $1200$ rated movies. The ratings ranges from one to five stars and are mapped to integers $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$. We will set $X[u,i]=0$ whenever an entry is missing.\\

In a realistic setting, most of the entries of $X$ are missing. For this reason, we define $C_{u}$ as the set of movies (column indexes) that user $u$ has rated and $H_{u}$ as its complement (the set of remaining unwatched/unrated movies we wish to predict ratings for). We use $\left|C_{u}\right|$ to denote the number of observed rating values from user $u$. From the point of view of our mixture model, each user $u$ is an example $x^{(u)}=x[u,:]$. But since most of the coordinates of $x^{(u)}$ are missing, we need to focus the model during training on just the observed portion. To this end, we use $x_{C_{u}}^{(u)}=\left\{x_{i}^{(u)}: i \in C_{u}\right\}$ as the vector of only observed ratings. If columns are indexed as $\{0, \ldots, d-1\}$, then a user $u$ with a rating vector $x^{(u)}=(5,4,0,0,2)$, where zeros indicate missing values, has $C_{u}=\{0,1,4\}, H_{u}=\{2,3\}$, and $x_{C_{u}}^{(u)}=(5,4,2)$.

$$
[X]_{1200 \times 1200}
$$

We have four additionals vector/matrices to initialize for the E.M. algorithm:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The prior on each gaussians that is set uniformly
    $$
    p = [0.086, ..., 0.086]_{12 \times 1}
    $$
    \item We set the mean of each gaussian as a random subset of twelve observations that we denote as X tilde
    $$
    \mu = [\Tilde{X}]_{12 \times 1200}
    $$
    \item Regarding the variance 
    $$
    var_j = ( [X]_{1200 \times K} -\mu_j)^2.mean()
    $$
    \item The posterior distribution is that we assign a uniform probability for every single observations on all the gaussians
    $$
    [post]_{1200, 12}=    \begin{pmatrix}
    0.0833 & 0.0833 & ... \\
    0.0833 & 0.0833 & ... \\
    ... & ... & ... 
    \end{pmatrix}
    $$
\end{itemize}

\textbf{(ii) E-step.}
The marginal

$$
P\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} \mid \theta\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_{j}~ \mathcal{N}\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{\left|C_{u}\right| \times\left|C_{u}\right|}\right)
$$

The posterior probability, this is the soft assignment of cluster $j$ to data point $u$

$$
P\left(y=j \mid x_{C_{u}}^{(u)}\right) = p(j \mid u)=\frac{p(u \mid j) \cdot p(j)}{p(u)}=\frac{p(u \mid j) \cdot p(j)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} p(u \mid j) \cdot p(j)}=\frac{\pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)}
$$

To minimize numerical instability we re-implement the E-step in the log-domain. Let $f(u, i)=\log \left(\pi_{i}\right)+\log \left(N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(i)}, \sigma_{i}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)\right)$. Then, in terms of $f$, the log posterior is:
$$
\begin{aligned}
\ell(j \mid u) = & \log (p(j \mid u))=\log \left(\frac{\pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)}\right) \\
&=\log \left(\pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)\right)-\log \left(\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)\right) \\
&=\log \left(\pi_{j}\right)+\log \left(N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)\right)-\log \left(\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp \left(\log \left(\pi_{j} N\left(x_{C_{u}}^{(u)} ; \mu_{C_{u}}^{(j)}, \sigma_{j}^{2} I_{C_{u} \times C_{u}}\right)\right)\right)\right) \\
&=f(u, j)-\log \left(\sum_{j=1}^{K} \exp (f(u, j))\right)
\end{aligned}
$$




\textbf{(iii) M-step.} First, note that, by decomposing the multivariate spherical Gaussians into univariate spherical Gaussians as before, we can write, if $l \in C_{u}$ :


$$
\widehat{\mu_{l}^{(k)}}=\frac{\sum_{u=1}^{n} p(k \mid u) \delta\left(l, C_{u}\right) x_{l}^{(u)}}{\sum_{u=1}^{n} p(k \mid u) \delta\left(l, C_{u}\right)} .
$$

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat{\sigma_{k}^{2}} &=\frac{1}{\sum_{u=1}^{n}\left|C_{u}\right| p(k \mid u)} \sum_{u=1}^{n} p(k \mid u)\left\|x_{C_{u}}^{(u)}-\widehat{\mu_{C_{u}}^{(k)}}\right\|^{2}, \\
\widehat{\pi_{k}} &=\frac{1}{n} \sum_{u=1}^{n} p(k \mid u) . \quad \text{sum of the rows for a fixed column}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\delta\left(i, C_{u}\right)=1$ if $i \in C_{u}$ and $\delta\left(i, C_{u}\right)=0$ if $i \notin C_{u}$. Note that it is possible to vectorize the norm of the variance by calculating

$$
\begin{aligned}
SSE &= [tilde\_vector - \mu] \times delta
\end{aligned}
$$

where $[tilde\_vector]_{12\times 1200}$ is a matrix where we repeat 12 times the same observation. And recall that $[\mu]_{12 \times 1200}$ are 12 different vectors. On top of that we multiply by $delta$ to set to zero the unknown elements

$$
[delta]_{1200 \times 1200} = \begin{pmatrix}
True & True & .. \\
True & False & ..\\
.. & .. & ..
\end{pmatrix}
$$

Finally the norm is equivalent to $sum(axis=1)$\\


\textbf{(iv) Fill-in Matrix.} After several rounds of $E-step$ and $M-step$ we end up calculating the missing values replacing the zero's in the matrix $X$.

$$
X_{filled} := \delta X+ (1 - \delta) \langle [post]_{1200, 12} \times [\mu]_{12 \times 1200} \rangle
$$

Where $post$ is the posterior probability matrix and $\mu$ the mean matrix for our 12 clusters. Passing the $1-\delta$ matrix will set all known ratings to zero retaining only those that are unknowns.

\subsubsection{Multinomial VAE}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

\textbf{Prerequisite (Multinomial Likelihood).} To start off recall the pdf of the multinomial distribution

$$
\begin{aligned}
f(x_1, x_2, .., x_n) &= \frac{n!}{x_1!x_2!..x_k!} p_1^{x_1} p_{2}^{x_2}... p_{k}^{x_k}\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i =n \quad and ~~~ \sum_{i=1}^n p_i = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

To tease out the log likelihood we just pass the log to the pdf

$$
L(p_1, p_2, .., p_n\mid x) = log(\frac{n!}{x_1!x_2!..x_k!}) + \sum_{i=1}^{k}x_i log(p_i)
$$

\textbf{Example (Numerical to do).}\\

\textbf{Definition (User-item interaction matrix).} Let $u \in \{1, .., U \}$ to index users and $i \in \{1, .., I \}$ to index items. The interaction matrix is the click matrix 

$$
X \in \mathbb{N}^{U \times I}
$$

The lower case $x_u = [x_{u1}, .., x_{uI}]^T \in \mathbb{N}^I$ is a bag-of-words vector with the number of clicks for each item from user $u$. \\

\textbf{VAE-CF.}\\
\textbf{(i) Inference.} For each user $u$, the model starts by sampling a $K$-dimensional latent representation $z_u$ from the standard gaussian prior. \\

\textbf{(ii) Generative.} The latent representation $z_u$ is transformed via a non-linear function $f_{\theta}(\cdot) \in \R^I$ to produce a probability distribution over $I$ items $\pi(z_u)$. From which the click history $x_u$ is assumed to have been drawn:

$$
\pi(z_u) \approx exp\{ f_{\theta}(z_u), z_u \sim N(0,I_k)  \}
$$

With $x_u \sim Multi(N_u, \pi(z_u))$

![[vae-1.png]]



\caption{Visually the first user is mapped to $z_u$ and then mapped to the first row of the output matrix}



\textbf{(iii) Loss function.} The objective of Multi-VAE for a single user $u$ is:

$$
\mathscr{L}(\theta, \phi) = \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}(z_u \mid x_u)} [log~p_{\theta}(x_u \mid z_u)] - \beta KL(q_{\phi}(z_u \mid x_u) ||p(z_u))
$$

Note that:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The output of $f_{\theta}(\cdot)$ is normalized via a softmax function to produce a probability vector $\pi(z_u)$.
    \item The multinomial log-likelihood for user $u$ (conditioned on the latent representation) is
    $$
    log~p_{\theta}(x_u \mid z_u) = \sum_i x_{ui} log~\pi_i(z_u)
    $$
\end{itemize}


\textbf{- Trick.} Regarding the parameter $\beta$ we have to rely on two strategies:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We first propose using $\beta \neq 1$ because we are no longer optimizing a lower bound on the log-marginal likelihood.
    \item Secondly if $\beta <1$ it entails that the model is less able to generate novel user histories. Which is what we aim we are solely interested to obtain good recommendation not do good on the generative model.
    \item Heuristic: We start $\beta=0$ that we gradually increase until we reach $one$. And we record the best $\beta$.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{- Prediction.}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item First train the Multi-VAE 
    \item Give a user click history $x$, then we rank all the items based on the un-normalized predicted multinomial probability $f_{\theta}(z_u)$
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Algorithm.}\\
\textbf{(i) Inputs.} Let $[train\_data]_{N \times n\_items}$ where $N$ is the total number of users and $n\_items$ the quantity of unique items. The ratings ranges from $\{1, 2, .., 5 \}$. Yet we constraint is to $\{ 4, 4.5, 5 \}$. More precisely $N=116,477$ number of users, $n\_items=20,101$ number of items. \\
\textbf{(ii) Building the model.} To build the model we have to define the dimension of the inference layers

$$
p\_dims = [200, 600, n\_items] = [200, 600, 20101]
$$

And the generative layers

$$
q\_dims = [n\_items, 600, 200] = [20101, 600, 400]
$$

During the $z$ phase we do

$$
z = std \times [\epsilon]_{batch \times 200} + \mu, \quad \epsilon \sim N(0,1)
$$


![[vae-2.png]]


\textbf{(iii) Prediction.} When it comes to prediction we will look solely at the top five predictions for example.\\

\textbf{(iv) Loss.} Don't forget that the reconstruction loss is a categorical loss.
